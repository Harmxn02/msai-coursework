We propose three controls: a pre-decision explainability layer, mandatory deliberation with IHL checks, and Glass Box governance, each mirroring the CHOF layers as proposed by \citet{info12090385}.
At the technical layer, SHAP and Grad-CAM provide evidence recommendations, while \citet{miller2018explanationartificialintelligenceinsights}'s contrastive framing and \citet{liao2020questioning}'s Question Bank translate feature scores into the Why, Why-not, and What-if that IHL requires.
At the socio-technical layer, a hard interface lock enforces deliberation time, replacing the single-button approval that enables rubber-stamping. % oorspronkelijk citaat: info12090385
At the governance layer, \citet{ijcai2019p802}'s Glass Box defines compliance norms at the input-output level, creating an audit trail that enables reviewing without accessing classified internals.
Each control is mutually dependent: the XAI panel is meaningless without deliberation time, and without the Glass Box audit trail, nothing is verifiable \textemdash{} consistent with \citet{morley2020what}'s finding that post-hoc explainability is typically only implemented at the testing stage, leaving deployment and monitoring underdeveloped.
\subsection{Stakeholder impact analysis}
For IDF operators, contrastive XAI explanations and mandatory deliberation locks would restore genuine freedom of action: they would receive the information necessary to critically evaluate each recommendation, and the interface lock would guarantee sufficient time to do so. Should a strike ever be investigated by an independent auditor or international tribunal, operators could demonstrate precisely how they arrived at their decision, rather than having no legal defence.
For civilians, mandating civilian density and protected-site proximity as structural interface requirements would embed their interests directly into the decision workflow. Combined with IHL checklist completion and appropriate handling of low-confidence recommendations, this could meaningfully reduce civilian casualties, while the audit trail would enable accountability for unjustified harm.
For the international community, the Glass Box framework would enable independent verification of a classified system's compliance record within an IHL-accountability context, shifting responsibility diffusion towards a structured and auditable chain of authorisation.
\subsection{Concluding remarks and future perspectives}
These recommendations carry limitations. The Glass Box approach depends on political will to establish an independent audit body \textemdash{} something Israel's non-ratification of Additional Protocol I suggests may be difficult to achieve. Deliberation standards and volume constraints would also reduce operational tempo, a primary advantage motivating AWS adoption.
Habsora sets a precedent for how AI targeting systems are evaluated under IHL. There is a clear need for international binding frameworks and global standardisation of pre-deployment review requirements for AWS. As AI continues to advance, ethical and legal considerations must be integrated into the development of autonomous weapon systems to ensure accountability and protect human rights.
