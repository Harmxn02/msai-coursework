\subsection{Technical Layer}
Habsora is an ensemble of deep learning models processing multimodal data~\citep{abraham_2024, dwoskin_2024}. Such architectures are inherently opaque; \citet{arrieta2019explainableartificialintelligencexai} characterise this as the accuracy/interpretability trade-off, where deep ensembles maximise predictive performance at the cost of transparency. While post-hoc explainability methods exist, reports on Habsora's operational use indicate that operators were not required to examine the basis for the system's recommendations, reducing human review to a cursory verification that a target was male~\citep{abraham_2024}.


This opacity directly links to legal consequences. \citet{ootlaws} establish that IHL's distinction principle requires positive identification of a target as a military objective, alongside a proportionality assessment that is understood by the user. Without any interpretability, the legal review is effectively empty.

These failures map directly onto the highest-ranked principles identified by \citet{khan2021ethicsaisystematicliterature}. The opacity of Habsora's recommendations and the absence of operator-facing explanations constitute a direct violation of \textbf{P-01} (transparency), while the inability to trace or attribute targeting decisions undermines \textbf{P-03} (accountability). The principle violations are further compounded by two structural challenges: the lack of technical understanding among decision-makers (\textbf{CH-06}), which prevents operators from critically evaluating system outputs, and the absence of binding legal frameworks (\textbf{CH-09}), which removes any external obligation to address these gaps. Together, this creates a system that cannot provide the context necessary for meaningful human understanding or oversight.

\subsection{Socio-technical}

Value Sensitive Design (VSD) is a framework for incorporating human values into the design of technical systems in a principled and structured way~\citep{friedman2008vsd, friedman2013vsd}. Central to VSD is the distinction between direct stakeholders, those who interact with a system, and indirect stakeholders, those affected by it without ever using it. In the case of Habsora, those indirect stakeholders are civilians in the target environment, whose values were not kept in mind during development. They remain non-consenting parties who face the consequences of the system's use without recourse, reflecting a large asymmetry between those operating the system and those subjected to it.

For IDF operators, the conditions necessary for genuine human oversight are structurally absent. \citet{mhcof} argue that meaningful human control requires both sufficient information and meaningful freedom of action. Habsora undermines both: operators receive recommendations without transparency into the underlying reasoning, and short decision windows leave almost no room for critical evaluation. \citet{miller2018explanationartificialintelligenceinsights} shows that without understanding what went into a decision, the explainee cannot meaningfully interrogate it. Combined with the large volume of targets the system generates, what would normally require extended deliberation and proportionality assessments is reduced to a button press. Former IDF operators have described this as `rubber-stamping'~\citep{kozlovski2024algorithms} \textemdash{} those with authority merely approving algorithmic recommendations \textemdash{} confirming the presence of automation bias, where operators over-rely on AI outputs under time pressure~\citep{parasuraman2010complacency}.

The consequence is that IDF operators formally bear moral and legal responsibility for strikes they cannot meaningfully evaluate, while the system's opacity diffuses accountability. This prevents the human control that International Humanitarian Law demands, and that \citet{mhcof} identify as a precondition for attributing responsibility.

\subsection{Governance}
Article 36 of Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions requires states to perform legal reviews of new weapons systems~\citep{icrc_api_article36_1977}. Israel is one of the few countries that have not ratified Protocol I~\citep{icrc_api_stateparties}, meaning they are not obligated to consider the legal consequences of systems like Habsora.

UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of AI urges member states against delegating life-or-death decisions to automated systems without meaningful human oversight~\citep{unesco2021recommendation}, but this instrument is non-binding. Israel's stated position that existing IHL is sufficient to govern autonomous weapon systems and that no new regulatory framework is required~\citep{israel_laws_2024, automatedresearch_israel} suggests that such recommendations carry little institutional weight in its operational decisions.
% https://automatedresearch.org/news/state_position/israel/

Once deployed, establishing accountability becomes difficult. Under IHL, operators of such systems are legally responsible for how weapons are used, even when autonomous functions are involved~\citep{icrc_api_article86_1977}, and are judged on what they knew at the time~\citep{henckaerts2005customary}. In practice, responsibility is spread across developers, the algorithm, the receiving officer, and the approving commander~\citep{aimag.v40i1.2848}. Lavender's documented 10\% error tolerance in targeting recommendations suggests Habsora may carry a similarly high error tolerance. Real-time monitoring during active conflict compounds the issue further.

After deployment, review processes are structurally undermined. The UN Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory has requested access to Gaza to investigate and preserve evidence, but Israel has refuses to cooperate~\citep{uncoiopt_2024}. The opacity of Habsora further compounds this: without interpretable audit trails, it is not possible to establish in retrospect whether a casualty resulted from an AI recommendation or a human decision.
